1. We can move fast — our data proves it. Across 24 US retina programs, Ora has activated up to 22 sites in a single month (Study 2) and routinely achieves 10–18 SIVs per month during peak activation. 30 sites in 30 days is not realistic — but 30 sites in 8–10 weeks is achievable based on our historical performance.

2. We already know these sites. Ora has 177 US retina sites with SIV history, 102 with multi-study experience. We can waive formal feasibility questionnaires for sites where we have historical activation, enrollment, and screen-fail data — replacing a 3–4 week process with site confirmation during qualification. This is a structural time advantage no other bidder can offer.

The 25th Percentile Argument for Oculgen

Oculgen asks: 30 sites activated in 30 days.
Our data says: Our 10th percentile for retina is 43 days — achievable for individual sites, but not for 30 simultaneously. Our 25th percentile for retina is 63 days (2.1 months) — and we can commit to this across all 30 sites by applying three structural advantages:

1. Skip-Feasibility (saves ~14 days). For sites in our network, we already have historical activation timelines, enrollment performance, screen-fail rates, PI capability, and IRB pathway data. Instead of issuing 30 cold feasibility questionnaires (2–3 week cycle), we pre-populate site dossiers from our database and have each site confirm/update during a qualification call. This compresses Site Selected → Contract from 35 days (retina P25) to an estimated 20 days.

2. Site selection bias toward repeat fast performers. The table above shows 13 retina sites that have activated in the top quartile across multiple Ora retina studies (nAMD, GA, DME, RVO) — these aren't outliers, they're operationally fast organizations. We select 30 sites from this pool plus our broader retina network, weighted by activation speed, nAMD enrollment history, and competitive landscape.

3. Parallel activation tracks. Central IRB (7 days at P25), contracts initiated concurrently with feasibility confirmation (not sequentially), and SIV scheduling locked during IRB review.

Projected result: 25th percentile with skip-feasibility efficiencies = ~48–55 days to SIV for the fastest wave, ~63 days for the full 30-site network. First SIVs by Week 6–7, all 30 by Week 9–11. This is 1.5× faster than retina industry median and gets Oculgen operational before October 2026.

Skip-Feasibility Differentiator

For the top 40 sites above, Ora already has: historical activation timelines (site selected → SIV → FSI), enrollment performance (patients screened, enrolled, PSM), screen-fail rates, PI and coordinator contact information, MSA status, and IRB pathway history.

Proposal positioning: Instead of issuing 30 feasibility questionnaires (3–4 weeks), Ora proposes a data-driven site qualification process: present each site with their own historical dossier, have the site confirm/update during a qualification call, and move directly to activation. This saves ~3 weeks from the critical path — time Oculgen cannot afford to lose given the October 2026 Part B start.

This is a capability no other bidder can replicate without building it from scratch.
Strategy Notes — Accelerating Treatment-Naïve Recruitment
1. Site selection IS the recruitment strategy. Weight site selection toward: (a) sites with the richest newly-diagnosed referral pipelines (optometry networks, health system integrations, retina centers with high new-patient volume), (b) sites with demonstrated fast activation from Ora data, (c) sites with less treatment-naïve competition from the major competitor trial.

2. Pre-screening is non-negotiable. Current 86–90% screen fail is destroying efficiency. A central pre-screening/contact center that triages on naïve status, lesion characteristics, and willingness before the formal screening visit is the single highest-impact intervention. Target: reduce on-site screen fail from ~88% to ≤50%.

3. Competitor screen-fail capture. The major competitor (screening ~3,000 for ~2,000) will generate ~1,000 screen fails + decliners. Build a systematic capture pathway: site-level protocols for offering STAT to competitor screen-fails, geographic positioning near competitor sites but not overlapping, and rapid consent-to-screen conversion for warm referrals.

4. Compensation must be competitive. Treatment-naïve nAMD patients have an approved standard of care readily available. Site and patient compensation must reflect the opportunity cost of delaying treatment to participate — if the competitor pays more, patients will go there.

5. Speed-to-consent wins. The naïve window closes the moment a patient receives their first anti-VEGF injection. Every day from diagnosis to consent is a day the patient might start standard therapy. The recruitment infrastructure must minimize this gap: same-week referral-to-screening, streamlined consent, and site coordinator training on the urgency of the naïve window.
